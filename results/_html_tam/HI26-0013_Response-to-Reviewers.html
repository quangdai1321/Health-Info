Reviewer 2

R2.1 — "The baseline is confounded by formatting: 97.3% of baseline outputs used display names instead of canonical codes, so part of its 0.0% safe output may reflect format mismatch rather than content errors."

This was the comment we found most important, because the 0.0% figure carries much of the paper's argument. We tested it directly rather than arguing from design.

Every baseline output was re-scored after mapping each returned analyte name back to its canonical code through the alias table, which eliminates code-format errors completely (1,253 names were remapped across the 150 panels). Results, now in Section 5 and Table 3:

Contradictory wording56.0%56.0%

Removing formatting entirely moves safe output from zero to one panel in 150. Direction mismatch rises slightly, because items previously counted as fabricated analytes are now resolved to a real analyte and judged on their stated direction. The baseline fails on content, not on format. The re-scoring script is src/cham_lai_bo_loi_dinh_dang.py.

R2.2 — "One stated contribution (the unit-normalisation failure) has no quantitative results in Section 5."

Correct, and now fixed. We added a deterministic experiment on the same 150 panels that needs no model call: compare the labels produced with unit conversion against the labels a system without conversion would produce. Results, now in Section 5:

  • 143 of 2,118 analyte readings (6.8%) were reported in a unit of different magnitude.
  • Without conversion, 135 of those 143 (94.4%) receive the wrong label.
  • 97 of 150 panels (64.7%) contain at least one such error.
  • All 135 are false alarms at the most severe level; none run in the safer direction.

Script: src/thu_nghiem_don_vi.py; output: results/thu_nghiem_don_vi.json.

R2.3 — "The Constrained configuration bundles rule-assigned labels, prompt constraints, and the JSON schema, so RQ2 cannot separate their contributions, and a validator-only configuration is missing."

Accepted, and this drove the main new experiment. Three further configurations were run on the same 150 panels with the same model and the same scoring code:

  • Labels only — rule-assigned labels and the JSON schema, with the content prohibitions removed from the system prompt.
  • Constraints only — content prohibitions and the JSON schema, with raw values and no labels.
  • Validator only — the baseline prompt unchanged, plus Layer 4: validation, regeneration up to the retry limit, and template fallback.

Results, now in Table 3 of the revised paper (% of panels, except the last column):

The three mechanisms turn out to do different jobs, and only one of them fixes correctness:

  1. The labels carry the correctness gain. Supplying them alone cuts direction mismatch

from 91.3% to 14.0% and false reassurance from 32.0% to 10.7%, and produces 38.7% safe output where the baseline produces none.

  1. The prompt constraints do not improve correctness at all. With no labels, direction

mismatch stays at 87.3% and false reassurance rises to 43.3%, above the baseline's 32.0%. Their one measurable effect is on vocabulary: forbidden terms fall from 79.3% to 35.3%. Instructing the model to be careful makes it speak more cautiously, not more correctly — which is precisely the failure mode the paper argues against, and we now have a number for it.

  1. The validator can guarantee safety without labels, but only by discarding the output.

Validator-only reaches 100.0% safe, the same as the full pipeline, yet 0.0% of panels are served by model text: all 150 fall back to templates after a mean of 3 regeneration attempts, at 95.5 s per panel. The full pipeline reaches the same 100.0% with 64.0% model text and lower latency. Layer 4's contribution is therefore the guarantee; what the labels and constraints buy is the share of panels that keep a generated explanation.

This also answers the implicit question of what "Constrained" adds over "Labels only": 9.3 further points of safe output (38.7% → 48.0%) and a drop in forbidden terms from 46.0% to 15.3%, with direction mismatch and false reassurance only slightly improved. The ablation is reproducible with python src/run_experiment.py --config chi_nhan|chi_rang_buoc|chi_kiem_chung; the comparison table is built by src/phan_tich_ablation.py and stored in results/bang_ablation.json.

One implementation note, reported for completeness: the constraints-only configuration produced outputs whose JSON structure was valid but whose field types were not what the validator expected, which raised exceptions during scoring. Rather than drop those panels we added a wrapper that records them as a malformed_structure violation, so an unscoreable output counts as unsafe rather than disappearing from the denominator.

Accepted twice over: we narrowed the claims and ran a larger model.

The abstract now states that the results bound what locally served open models do, and the introduction no longer uses one tested model as evidence about general-purpose assistants.

R2.5 — "Patient-friendliness is not measured: no readability score, comprehension test, or human rating, and 36% of panels receive templates that are no better than the report."

Accepted, and we did not try to cover it with a proxy metric. The abstract and Section 6 now state that the evaluation establishes consistency with assigned labels only, and that readability and comprehension remain unmeasured. The 36% template share is reported in the abstract, in Section 5 and in Section 6 wherever the 100% figure appears.

R2.6 — "Limited novelty: rules plus an LLM paraphraser plus a validator with template fallback is a well-known guardrail and data-to-text pattern, and the related work does not engage with that literature."

Accepted. Section 2 now places the architecture in that literature and states plainly that it contributes nothing architecturally, citing NeMo Guardrails for programmable rails around a model, Gatt and Krahmer for the separation of content selection from realisation, Wiseman et al. for neural generators misstating facts present in their input, and Thomson and Reiter for accuracy evaluation in data-to-text. The contribution is restated more narrowly: false reassurance measured as its own class, the cost of the guarantee reported alongside it, and a quantified failure of the deterministic layer under Vietnamese unit conventions.

R2.7 — "The severity factors (0.15–0.50) were set heuristically, so the claim that classification is exact by construction holds only relative to unvalidated thresholds."

Accepted. The sentence now reads that classification is exact with respect to the configured intervals and severity factors, and explicitly not a claim that those thresholds are clinically validated. We also added a per-analyte provenance table (data/nguon_khoang_tham_chieu.csv) recording, for each of the 22 analytes, the reference interval, its source, the severity factor, and its validation status. A companion table (data/tu_cam_nguon.csv) records every forbidden-term pattern with the behaviour it blocks and its basis, which is the scope of medical practice: diagnosis and prescription are reserved to licensed practitioners under Vietnamese Law 15/2023/QH15, and EFLM advises against diagnostic use of language models on laboratory results. Both tables are checked against the running code by unit tests, so documentation and implementation cannot drift.

R2.8 — "No sex- or age-specific intervals, which matter clinically for haemoglobin and haematocrit."

Accepted. Section 6 now states this as a separate limitation with its consequence: a woman's result may be labelled abnormal against a male-derived interval. The provenance table marks haemoglobin, haematocrit and red cell count as sex-dependent.

Reviewer 1

We thank Reviewer 1 for a review detailed enough to act on point by point. Two of the twelve points changed our reading of our own results, and we report both outcomes even where they weaken the original claims. The title has been changed to the one the reviewer proposed.

R1.1 (Critical) — "Safe output is defined entirely by an internal validator, so the 100% figure is self-referential."

Accepted without reservation, and this is the single largest change to the paper.

The metric is renamed throughout. "Safe output" is now validator-pass rate, defined in Section 4.2 with the reason attached: the validator is both the filter and the measure, so a high rate shows that nothing was returned that violates the implemented checks, not that the output is clinically appropriate or comprehensible.

We then tested how little the metric distinguishes. A template-only configuration was added, which prints the Layer 2 labels through the fixed template and never calls a model. It passes on all 150 panels in 0.38 s for the whole benchmark, against 68.3 s per panel for the full pipeline. McNemar's test on the paired panels cannot separate the two (p = 1.000), and the same holds for validator-only. The paper now states this in Section 5 and in Section 6.2 rather than leaving it to be inferred: a pass rate defined by consistency with the rules is maximised by output the rules wrote, and what the pipeline adds over the template is the 64.0% of panels that keep generated prose, whose readability we did not measure.

We did not arrange blind expert rating before the camera-ready deadline, and we do not claim to have. Section 6.2 lists independent expert review and a patient comprehension study as the first items of future work, and the conclusion no longer describes the result as safety.

R1.2 (Critical) — "Monitor and seek care carry triage implications but rest on heuristic severity factors."

Accepted. Section 3.2 now states that the names denote deviation categories computed from the configured intervals, not triage advice, and that mapping a deviation category to patient-facing wording would require validation against a laboratory's critical-value policy and clinician review before deployment. The same qualification appears in Section 6.2.

We also made the thresholds auditable rather than only disclaimed. data/nguon_khoang_tham_chieu.csv records, for each of the 22 analytes, the reference interval, its source, the severity factor, whether the interval is sex-dependent, and its validation status, which is "not checked against literature" for every row. A unit test keeps the file in step with the running code. Appendix A.3 reproduces the table.

R1.3 (High) — "The synthetic generator is not described well enough to reproduce or to check for bias."

Accepted. Section 4.1 now gives the generator parameters and the distributions, and Appendix A supplies the rest: the generator is src/generate_data.py with n_per_group=50, seed=42, borderline_rate=0.15, so the benchmark regenerates exactly. Appendix A reports the panel-label split (50/50/50), analytes per panel (10–18, mean 14.1, 2,118 readings), abnormal analytes per panel (0–4, mean 1.5), the high/low split (124/102), the 46 panels with a borderline value, the per-analyte abnormality counts, the reference interval and severity factor per analyte, the unit conversion factors, and the alias table. data/synthetic_panels.json is supplied as generated.

We also state what the reviewer suspected. Appendix A notes that no reading lacks a unit and no panel lacks an analyte name, because the generator emits structured panels only; Section 6.2 carries this as a limitation rather than a footnote.

R1.5 (High) — "Patient comprehension is not evaluated, so the patient-friendly claim is unsupported; otherwise change the title."

Accepted, and we took the reviewer's second option because we could not run a comprehension study before the deadline and did not want to keep a claim we had not measured. The title is now:

A Rule-Constrained Large Language Model Pipeline for Rule-Consistent Explanation of Vietnamese Complete Blood Count Results

This follows the reviewer's own wording and narrows the scope twice over: rule-consistent explanation instead of patient-friendly interpretation, and complete blood count instead of blood test, which also answers R1.8. The abstract, introduction and conclusion were rewritten to match, and Section 6.2 states that readability, comprehension, perceived anxiety and actionability were not measured.

R1.6 (High) — "No confidence intervals, no repeated runs, no decoding parameters."

Addressed on all three counts.

Every proportion in Sections 4 and 5 now carries a 95% Wilson confidence interval, chosen over Wald because several rates are 0% or 100%, where Wald gives a zero-width interval. Configurations are compared with McNemar's test, the paired test this design calls for, since all configurations see the same 150 panels. Two of those comparisons changed what we could claim: constraints over labels is not significant (p = 0.082), and template-only against full is indistinguishable (p = 1.000).

Decoding parameters are now in Section 4.2: Ollama, Q4_K_M quantisation, temperature 0.2, 2,048-token limit, JSON output mode, no fixed seed.

Repeated runs were carried out for the constrained configuration, five times on the same panels. We chose that configuration because its pass rate is neither 0% nor 100% and therefore has room to vary; repeating a configuration pinned at 100% by construction would measure little on the same hours of compute.

The single run reported in Table 2 (48.0%) sits at the low end of ordinary variation, and no per-class rate moves by more than about three points. The more interesting result is at the panel level: across the five runs only 31 of 150 panels pass every time and 37 fail every time, while 82 (54.7%) pass in some runs and fail in others. Aggregate rates on 150 panels are reproducible to within a few points, but an individual explanation is not reproducible at all. R1.7 (High) — "The validator is not specified well enough to audit."

Accepted. Appendix B now specifies it in full: the algorithm in pseudo-code, the normalisation applied before matching (accent stripping and reference-phrase masking, with the comparator rule that governs the masking), the complete pattern tables for status words, false reassurance and forbidden terms, and a section on what the checks cannot see. The appendix is generated from the running code by src/dung_phu_luc.py, so it cannot drift from the implementation.

On precision and recall we report what we have rather than an estimate we cannot support. Appendix B.5 documents a systematic false positive we found while preparing this response: the contradictory-wording check masks interval-naming phrases only when a comparator precedes them, so a sentence that quotes the interval as a fact is read as a status claim. A pattern scan identifies 50 of the 206 flagged sentences in the baseline log (24.3%) as candidates for this mistake. We left the behaviour unchanged, because retuning the validator after seeing the results would invalidate comparisons already scored with it, and we state the direction of the bias: it inflates the fallback rate and understates the model, and does not inflate the pass rate. Section 6.2 now carries this. A proper precision and recall estimate needs human-annotated statements and is listed as future work.

R1.8 (High) — "Scope is wider than the experiments: real reports are not structured CBC panels."

Accepted. The title now says complete blood count, the abstract and Section 6.2 state that only structured CBC panels are supported, and the introduction no longer generalises from one local model to hosted assistants. Parsing, OCR, missing reference intervals, multi-panel reports and age- or sex-specific intervals are named as untested.

R1.9 (Medium) — "Runtime analysis is insufficient for deployment."

Addressed from the existing logs, and in the course of doing so we found and corrected an error of our own. The submitted version described the test machine as "an Intel Core i5-9400F with 24 GB RAM and no GPU", and Section 6.1 argued that the pipeline "avoids GPU cost for provincial hospitals". That was wrong. The machine has an NVIDIA GTX 1050 Ti with 4 GB, and we verified by re-measurement that Qwen2.5-3B at Q4_K_M occupies about 3 GB and is offloaded to the card in full, at 100% GPU utilisation. Every 3B timing in the paper was obtained with GPU acceleration. Section 4.2 now states the actual hardware and the offload split, and Section 6.1 now argues from a consumer graphics card released in 2016 rather than from no card at all. The 7B model does not fit in 4 GB and runs roughly half on the card, which is why it is 2.4 times slower rather than 2.3 times as its parameter count would suggest.

Section 5 now reports, for the full pipeline, 68.3 s per panel with SD 33.7 and p50/p95/p99 of 66.4/121.5/134.1 s, against a baseline mean of 38.9 s (p95 63.6), and Section 6.1 quotes the 95th percentile rather than the mean alone. Per-panel attempts are 1.97 on average.

We also answer where the cost goes. Re-scoring the outputs Layer 4 rejected shows that of the 54 panels ending in a template, 66.7% had omitted an analyte, 38.9% contained a forbidden term, 29.6% misstated a direction and 18.5% falsely reassured. Omission, not vocabulary, is what regeneration is mostly fighting.

Peak memory, token counts and concurrency behaviour are not reported; we did not instrument them during the runs and did not want to quote figures from a later, differently loaded machine.

R1.10 (Medium) — "No qualitative error analysis."

Accepted. Section 5 now carries a paragraph of real instances, and Appendix C tabulates one worked example per error class with the panel, the rule label, the analyte, the generated text and the reason Layer 4 rejected it. Every example is taken from the stored run logs; none is constructed. The false-reassurance example is the one the reviewer would expect: on a monitor panel, the baseline closing statement opens "Tất cả các chỉ số đều nằm trong khoảng bình thường" and then names two abnormal analytes.

R1.11 (Medium) — "Related work does not position the contribution precisely enough."

Accepted. Section 2 now engages the guardrail and data-to-text literature directly, citing NeMo Guardrails for programmable rails, Gatt and Krahmer for the separation of content selection from realisation, Wiseman et al. for neural generators misstating facts present in their input, and Thomson and Reiter for accuracy evaluation in data-to-text, and states plainly that the architecture contributes nothing new. The contribution paragraph is rewritten around the three gaps the reviewer identified: clinical judgement externalised from the model, semantic validation distinguished from structural validation, and false reassurance measured separately.

R1.12 (Medium) — "Absolute claims should be softened."

Accepted. "Produced no unsafe output" is now "left no output that violated the implemented checks"; "the pipeline never returns unverified text" is "no unvalidated model text is returned"; "the guarantee supplied by Layer 4" is "what Layer 4 enforces".
